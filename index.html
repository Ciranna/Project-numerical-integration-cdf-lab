Interpretation and limitations

Accuracy: absolute error is |estimate − reference|. Small error is better; timings alone do not establish accuracy.

Cost: Simpson and trapezoid use n + 1 PDF evaluations; Taylor uses k series terms; the erf polynomial uses a fixed formula. These counts are algorithmic proxies, not identical hardware operations.

Lebesgue: the approximation uses 4,096 domain samples plus value bins. It is not an exact computation of preimage measures. The Riemann method uses n samples, so this is not a comparison at equal evaluation budget.

Measurement: warm-up, multiple batches and the median reduce timing noise but do not eliminate browser JIT, scheduling or thermal effects. The benchmark executes synchronously and can briefly block the page.

Reference probabilities are tabulated values, not results of the compared algorithms. The full theoretical discussion is in docs/ACCURACY_PERFORMANCE_EN.md.

Conclusions & Findings — Homework 5

Interpretation of both exercises, numerical evidence, and limitations. This page summarizes experiments; it is not a mathematical proof based on numerical results alone.

EXERCISE 1

Riemann and Lebesgue

Riemann partitions the domain; Lebesgue partitions function values and measures their preimages. Both integral definitions agree for Riemann-integrable functions, even though their numerical approximations have different sources of error.

EXERCISE 2

Normal CDF

Simpson's rule gives high accuracy with moderate integration partitions. Taylor can reach near machine precision at moderate x, while the fixed erf approximation offers low computational cost with a nonzero approximation error.

Limitations & future improvements

What the experiments demonstrate — and what they do not.

Sampling vs. exact measure

Finite midpoint samples do not reproduce arbitrary measurable preimages. For the truncated 1/√x example, the numerical estimate can even exceed 2 although every exact truncated integral is below 2. Exact monotone convergence is not contradicted.

Floating-point stability

Double-precision arithmetic limits the meaningful digits. Alternating Taylor sums can lose accuracy at larger |x| through cancellation. A stable tail-specific method would improve extreme probabilities.

Timing uncertainty

Browser JIT compilation, timer resolution, CPU load, and thermal conditions influence measurements. Repeated batches and medians reduce but do not eliminate this variability.

Possible extensions

Adaptive quadrature, analytical or interval-based preimage measurement for selected functions, confidence intervals for benchmarks, and robust normal-tail algorithms would strengthen future versions.

Final conclusion

Numerical integration and probability computation require balancing mathematical validity, accuracy, convergence, and computational cost. Riemann and Lebesgue agree where both apply, but Lebesgue covers a broader class of functions. For the normal CDF, no single numerical method is optimal in every regime. Numerical experiments support theoretical understanding only when their discretization and floating-point limitations are made explicit.
